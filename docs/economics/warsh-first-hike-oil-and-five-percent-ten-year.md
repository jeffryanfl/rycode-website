# Warsh’s first rate increase, with oil above $100 and a ten-year near 5 percent

**Subhead:** On September 16, 2026 the Fed raised the funds rate by a quarter point. The long bond and the oil price were already high.

**Door:** Economics  
**URL:** `/economics/warsh-first-hike-oil-and-five-percent-ten-year/`  
**Status:** First read for Jeffrey (create-now from Macro Fact Sheet, 16 Sep 2026)

---

On September 16, 2026, the Federal Open Market Committee (the Fed body that sets the short-term policy rate) raised its target range for the federal funds rate (the overnight rate banks charge each other) by one quarter of a percentage point, to 3-3/4 to 4 percent. The vote was unanimous, twelve to zero. That is Kevin Warsh’s first rate increase as Chair under this meeting’s materials. It arrived clean on paper. The market’s reaction, the bond market’s level, and the oil price together say something harder. Policy can look decisive while the discount rate for equities stays high and energy keeps feeding inflation risk.

## What the statement actually said

The Committee did not publish a conventional forward-guidance path in the statement (a script that tells markets how many more cuts or hikes to expect). It said inflation remains elevated. It said the move supports a more timely return of inflation to the 2 percent goal. It said the Committee will deliver price stability. Those are firm words. They are not a calendar. Anyone who wanted a soft landing schedule from the statement itself did not get one.

Primary materials for the meeting, including the statement and the projection tables, sit on the Federal Reserve’s September 16, 2026 FOMC pages.

## What the projections imply

At selected FOMC meetings the Fed publishes the Summary of Economic Projections (SEP), a table of where Committee members see growth, unemployment, inflation, and the funds rate. The September 2026 SEP median for the year-end federal funds rate is 4.1 percent. The new target range’s midpoint is 3.875 percent. A median of 4.1 percent sits above that midpoint. In plain terms, the middle of the Committee still sees the policy rate ending the year a step higher than where it just set the range. That is hawkish relative to a “we’re done hiking” reading of a single quarter-point move.

The same SEP median for personal consumption expenditures (PCE) inflation in 2026 is 3.7 percent, up from 3.6 percent in the June projections. PCE is the Fed’s preferred inflation gauge. A tenth of a point higher may look small. As a revision to the Committee’s own inflation view after a hike, it is not a victory lap. It says the inflation path they are writing down did not improve; it edged the wrong way.

Prefer those medians as stated. Do not lean on informal vote-count paraphrases when the published median is the number that travels.

## Bonds and a five percent ten-year

On the same day, Treasury Constant Maturity Treasury (CMT) yields (the official daily curve the Treasury publishes) showed the two-year at 4.74 percent, the ten-year at 5.01 percent, and the thirty-year at 5.35 percent. A ten-year near five percent is not a trivia line for traders. It is the long risk-free rate that sits under how investors value stocks. When that rate is high, the same future cash flows are worth less today. Multiples (how many dollars of price investors pay per dollar of earnings) compress unless earnings grow enough to offset the higher discount rate.

Warsh’s first hike did not happen in a world of a three percent ten-year. It happened with the long bond already telling you duration is expensive.

## Oil still over one hundred after a down day

Crude fell on September 16. Brent settled at $105.83, down about 2.7 percent. West Texas Intermediate settled at $102.43, down about 3.2 percent. Both stayed above $100. The move followed reporting on extra crude from Oman and disruption risk around Yanbu. A down day from a high base is not relief in the policy sense. Oil above one hundred dollars a barrel keeps energy in the inflation story even when the daily tape prints green for crude bulls who wanted a bigger drop.

The hike and the oil print belong in the same frame. If energy stays elevated, the “timelier return to 2 percent” line has to fight a real input. Markets can celebrate a clean vote and still refuse to re-rate equities as if inflation risk had left the building.

## Stocks faded the day

Equities closed lower on FOMC day. The S&P 500 finished near 7,552, down about half a percent on the Yahoo Finance history print for that session. That is not a crash. It is also not a relief rally. A unanimous hike, hawkish statement language, an SEP funds-rate median above the new midpoint, a ten-year at five, and oil still over one hundred is a package that asks equity investors to pay for duration and inflation risk at the same time. The tape’s answer was a fade, not a melt-up.

## The structural point

Treat the day as three facts that stick together, not as theater about one Chair’s first move.

First, the policy step was clear and unanimous, with statement language that refuses a soft path and SEP medians that still see a higher year-end funds rate and slightly higher 2026 PCE inflation than June.

Second, the Treasury curve put the ten-year at five percent. That is a binding constraint on how rich stock valuations can stay if discount rates do not fall.

Third, oil above $100 after a down day means the energy channel into inflation did not leave with the press release.

If you own duration-sensitive equities, the question after Warsh’s first hike is not whether the Committee can raise rates by a quarter point. It already did. The question is whether five percent on the ten-year and triple-digit oil leave room for the multiples that priced a friendlier discount rate. On September 16 the bond market and the equity close answered first. The statement answered second. The oil market did not leave the room.
